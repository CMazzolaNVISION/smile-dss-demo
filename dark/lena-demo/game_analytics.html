DSS Prototype

The question
Does playing the SMILE game go with a better mood than usual, and which modules could be worth playing again?
What it uses
The change of mood between two app questionnaires of the same day, whether a game sublevel was played in between, and what else happened (stressful events, negative thinking, coping). The cohort's models are trained only on participants other than this one.
How it answers
First how much game data there is, then what goes with a better change for this participant, then which modules could be worth playing again, and finally whether a better score on a minigame goes with a better change.
How to read it
Statistics = mixed models, ML = a machine-learning model. Blue = better, orange = worse, faded = could be chance. These are associations, not causes: suggestions to discuss, not a prescription.

How the change of mood is measured

The unit is an interval: a mood observation and the previous mood observation of the same day (Core 1 → Core 2, Core 2 → Core 3, Core 3 → Evening; nights are never used). What is measured is the change of mood across it:

change = mood at this observation − mood at the previous observation (points, −3 to +3, + = better)

Someone who starts below their usual level tends to go back up anyway (and above, to come down): this is regression to the mean, not an effect of the game. So the model always compares changes that started at the same distance from the person's usual level: "better than usual" = better than their change in comparable intervals without it (e.g. without playing).

A game sublevel counts in the interval when its first completion (the one that awards the points) falls between the two observations; replays are not counted. Events, thinking and coping are the "since the last observation" answers of the observation that closes the interval.

All the terms are in one model together, so each number is net of the others: "played module 2" is compared with intervals with the same events, thoughts and strategies.

On one of Lena Demo's intervals: Overall mood on 21 Feb
  1. The interval: 21 Feb 13:58 → 17:11 UTC (3.2 h, same day); played module 5 (1 sublevels)
  2. Change: overall mood +0.5 at the previous observation → +2.0 now: +1.5 points (better).
  3. Starting point: their usual overall mood is +1.6 (average of 148 observations): the previous observation was 1.1 points below it, so a change up was expected anyway.
  4. Compared with usual: in their intervals without play that started at the same level, the change was +0.6; this one is +0.9 beyond the usual change.
The model does the same over all their intervals at once, and separates playing from what else happened in the interval (events, thinking, coping).

Can these labels be trusted? Statistics

We checked the labels on participants the model had not seen: their chance was computed from the other participants only, as for a new patient, and then we looked at whether their mood really changed better than usual when they played that module. Overall, 56 in 100 such cases were better than usual.

ⓘ About this view
How the labels were checked: the training participants are split in five groups; for each group, the chance of a better change is computed from the other four groups only (as for a new patient: the cohort average and how much people differ), and compared with what happened to that group's participants who played the module: did their mood change better than usual (their own estimate above 0)? For each label, the share of cases that were in fact better than usual. A label "holds" when this share is at least 5 points above (worth discussing) or below (went with a worse change) the overall share. Each person's own estimate rests on few play intervals, so it is noisy: the shares are pulled towards 50%.
  • "Worth discussing": about 7 in 10 were better than usual (the label holds)
    764 cases; the page gave them on average a 94% chance. A better change was more frequent than overall (56 in 100).
  • "Unsure": about 5 in 10 were better than usual
    1314 cases; the page gave them on average a 50% chance. As expected for "no idea".
  • "Went with a worse change": about 5 in 10 were better than usual (the label does not hold)
    370 cases; the page gave them on average a 10% chance. A better change was about as frequent as overall: do not read "went with a worse change" as a warning.

How the chance of a better change is computed

The number comes from the same model as the "This participant" tab. For a module Lena Demo played, the estimate (own data + cohort) and its uncertainty describe a bell curve of where their true effect lies; the chance is the part of that curve above 0 (better than usual). A wide curve centred near 0 gives about 50% (no idea).

For a module not played yet, there are no own data: the bell curve is the cohort average with how much people differ, the honest expectation for someone new. When people react very differently, this chance stays close to 50% even if the average is clearly positive.

At least 80% is highlighted as "worth discussing", at most 20% as "went with a worse change". These are associations: playing a module and feeling better may both follow something else.

Does a better score go with a better change? ML

A cohort model (Explainable Boosting Machine) learned from the training participants: each minigame is judged on its own score (0-100% of its points) in the interval where it was played, and the outcome is the change beyond the usual change. Each number: intervals with the top score vs the lowest score, in points of mood; full colour = clear or strong, faded or italics = could be chance or "small" (below 0.2 points).

ⓘ About this view
THE MODEL (machine learning): an Explainable Boosting Machine (interpret, regression, no interactions) per mood (overall mood, anxious, insecure, irritated), learned from the training participants (a cohort model).

DATA: every interval between two observations of the same day in which at least one minigame (a sublevel of the Game Performance sheet) was scored; each minigame's score in that interval = points won / points possible (0-100%), or nothing if it was not played there. OUTCOME: the change beyond the usual change = observed change minus the change expected from the person's intervals without play that started at the same level. INPUTS: the score of every minigame and a stressful event in the interval (adjustment). Only minigames with at least 20 scored intervals of 10 participants and two different scores enter.

The map shows "top vs lowest score" (the curve at the top score minus at the lowest score, points) for every minigame and the four moods: blue better, orange worse, faded or italics = uncertain or "small" (below 0.2 points).

How much do minigame scores matter? ML

Tested on participants the model never saw. The bar splits the change of mood beyond usual in play intervals into the part that the minigame scores account for (green) and everything else (grey). Most of it is everything else: the minigame score adds little on top of whether they played, what happened, how they thought and coped, and the ordinary ups and downs of a day that no question captures.

ⓘ About this view
Tested on participants the model never saw (participant-grouped cross-validation: each fifth of the participants is predicted by a model trained on the others).

MINIGAME SCORES (green part of the bar) = the R² of the predictions: the share of the variation of the change beyond usual, in play intervals, that the minigame scores account for. Everything else (grey) is what the scores do not account for: whether and what was played, events, thinking and coping, and the ordinary ups and downs of mood that no question captures. A value below 0 means the predictions for new participants were slightly worse than simply expecting no change beyond usual for everyone, i.e. no predictive value; it is shown as 0%.

RIGHT DIRECTION = among the intervals where the model expected at least 0.1 points of change, the share where the change went that way (50% = a coin flip).

WHAT GOES MOST WITH THE CHANGE: the terms of the main model with the largest clear cohort averages for that mood (points; + = better).

How the minigame model works

Data. Every interval between two observations of the same day in which at least one minigame was scored. Each minigame gets its score in that interval, 0-100% = points won / points possible (Game Performance rules), or nothing if it was not played there.

The outcome. The change beyond the usual change: the change observed minus the change expected from the same person's intervals without play that started at the same level. So differences between people, and the tendency to come back to the usual level, are already taken out.

The model. An Explainable Boosting Machine adds up one curve per minigame (score → part of the change), so each minigame can be read on its own; a stressful event in the interval is an extra input, to adjust for it. It is trained on all training participants together: the curves are the same for everyone.

How sure. The model is refitted five times, each time without a fifth of the participants. The 95% range of "top vs lowest score" comes from how much these five fits differ, and "same direction in" counts how often they agree. Many minigames are shown at once, so a single clear one should be read with care.

How much data is behind each part

ⓘ About this view
Green = enough data, amber = limited, red = too little. Intervals between two observations of the same day (overall mood): fewer than the eligibility threshold = too few, up to twice = limited. Intervals with a game sublevel: fewer than 3 = too few for own game estimates, 3-9 limited, 10 or more good. Share of each game estimate from the person's own data; events and strategies with an own estimate; role in training.
  • 106 intervals between two observations of the same day
    With overall mood answered at both observations. Fewer than 20 = too few, 20-39 = limited, 40 or more = good.
  • 16 of them with a game sublevel played
    Fewer than 3 = too few for this person's own game estimates (they lean on the cohort), 3-9 = limited, 10 or more = good. Sublevels are dated at their first completion (Game Performance rules); replays are not counted.
  • On average 6 % of each game estimate comes from this person's data
    The rest comes from the cohort average. Most people play a module in few intervals, so the cohort often carries most of the weight.
  • Own estimates for 9 of the 9 events, thoughts and coping strategies
    Each needs at least 5 intervals with it and 5 without (scores: 10 answers that vary); otherwise the page shows the cohort average for it.
  • This participant was kept out of training
    The results are a fair test, as for a new patient.

Models built on 2026-10-28 from 84 training participants, 7332 intervals (862 with play).
