ⓘ About this view
Between two ESM beeps of the same day, what goes with a BETTER CHANGE OF MOOD THAN USUAL: playing the game (which module), a stressful event, negative thinking, each coping strategy? Does a better score on a minigame go with a better change (most participants)? And which modules could be worth playing again?

THE CHANGE: mood at a beep minus mood at the previous beep of the same day, in points on the 1-7 scale (Core1 -> Core2, Core2 -> Core3, Core3 -> Evening; nights are never used). The model takes the mood at the previous beep into account, so "better than usual" = better than this person's change in comparable intervals without it.

Every number comes from a statistical model -- a linear mixed model, as in ESM Analytics -- in three steps: (1) the participant's own intervals; (2) the average of the training participants (same model, trained offline); (3) the two combined, weighted by how much data the participant has, with a 95% range and the chance that the change is better than usual.

Numbers follow the DSS rule: + = positive valence (better), − = negative valence (worse), whatever the mood; negative feelings are reversed (anxious (reversed) +0.5 = 0.5 points LESS anxious). Colors: blue = better, red = worse; hollow grey = uncertain; dashed = differs from most participants.

READ AS ASSOCIATIONS: the data are observational; the dashboard does not say that playing causes a change, and its suggestions are for discussion, not a prescription.

How Sofia Demo's happy changed between two beeps of the same day when they played the game (which module, how well) and when something else happened (a stressful event, negative thinking, coping). 112 intervals, 17 with a game sublevel played. These are associations, not causes.

How the numbers are made

ⓘ About this view
Interval: a mood beep (at least one mood item answered) and the participant's previous mood beep of the same day (entries before 03:30 belong to the previous day).

Game: the sublevels of the Game Performance dashboard, found and scored with its rules; a sublevel counts in the interval when its first completion (the one that awards the points) falls between the two beeps. Replays are not counted. "Played module k" = at least one sublevel of module k in the interval.

ESM: the "since the last beep" answers of the beep closing the interval, as in Mood Change Analytics: important or stressful event; coping with the event (score, only with an event); repetitive negative thinking (score); and the six coping strategies, each on its own (they are different strategies and are never merged): kept calm by breathing / relaxing muscles, thought about feelings over and over, talked to others about feelings, turned attention away, changed the way of thinking about the situation, tried not to show emotions. The event question is not asked at the Evening beep: a question not asked never removes an interval, it gets the person's average and so does not move any estimate.

Step 1, this person only: a regression of the mood at the beep on the mood at the previous beep and all the terms together (within-person). Step 2, the cohort: the average effect of the training participants and how much they differ (one random effect per term). The person's estimate = weight x own effect + (1 - weight) x cohort average. Chance of a better change = from the estimate and its uncertainty (normal distribution); for a module not played yet, from the cohort average and how much participants differ.

The unit is an interval: a mood beep and the previous mood beep of the same day. What is measured is the change of mood across it:

change = happy at this beep − happy at the previous beep  (points on the 1-7 scale)

Someone who starts below their usual level tends to go back up anyway. So the model always compares changes that started at the same distance from this person's usual level: "better than usual" = better than their change in comparable intervals without it (e.g. without playing). Then, as in the Context fingerprint, three steps:

1This person's data
Their own intervals only: with vs without playing a module (or an event, a strategy), at the same starting point, each adjusted for the others.
2Most participants
The same effect, averaged over 84 training participants (trained offline; no one else's data is read here).
3Combined estimate
1 and 2 weighted by how much data this person has, with a 95% range and the chance that the change is better than usual. Few play intervals → closer to most participants.

Minigame by minigame: does a better score go with a better change?

Most participants · score = share of the minigame's points won (0-100%, Game Scores rules)

Score and change of happy

ⓘ About this view
MOST PARTICIPANTS ONLY (a cohort model; the participant's own scores are marked). Does a better SCORE on a minigame go with a better change of mood?

Data: every interval between two beeps of the same day in which at least one minigame (a sublevel of the Game Performance sheet) was scored. Each minigame gets its score in that interval, 0-100% = points awarded / points possible (Game Performance scoring rules), or nothing if it was not scored there. Outcome: the change beyond the usual change = observed change minus the change expected from the person's intervals without play that started at the same level, so differences between people and the starting point are already taken out.

Model: an Explainable Boosting Machine (interpret, no interactions): one curve per minigame (score -> part of the change, points), each minigame judged on its own score, plus a stressful event in the interval as an adjustment. Only minigames with at least 20 scored intervals of 10 participants and two different scores enter.

"Top vs lowest score": the curve at the top score minus at the lowest score, with a 95% range from a grouped jackknife over the cross-validation folds (each fold leaves out a fifth of the participants). Filled = clear or strong; hollow grey = uncertain; "small" = below 0.2 points whatever the evidence. Hover: intervals, participants, and in how many folds the effect pointed the same way. "Curves": the whole curve of each minigame (marker size = intervals with that score); dotted line = this participant's score. Many minigames are shown: read a single clear one with care.

A cohort model (Explainable Boosting Machine): each minigame is judged on its own score, 0-100% of its points, in the interval where it was played. The outcome is the change beyond the usual change (differences between people and the starting point already taken out). Example: +0.3 = intervals with the top score had a change 0.3 points better than those with the lowest score. "Small" = below 0.2 points. On the right / dotted line: this participant's own score.

Tested on participants never seen in training: the model's error on the change beyond usual was 0.77 points vs 0.80 for "no difference" (4 % smaller), 893 play intervals of 84 participants. Small gains are expected: most of the change between two beeps is not about the game.
