DSS Prototype

The question
What goes with this participant's emotions in daily life: where they are, what they do, who they are with, what just happened, how they cope, and how they slept or played?
What it uses
The short questionnaires of the companion app, several times a day: emotions, situations, events, negative thinking and coping strategies, plus sleep and game play. The cohort's models are trained only on participants other than this one.
How it answers
From the cohort to the person: first what goes with each emotion for most participants and how much people differ, then the same for this participant, how emotions carry over between observations, and which inputs go with a mood better or worse than usual.
How to read it
Statistics = mixed models, ML = a machine-learning model. Blue = better, orange = worse, faded = could be chance. These are associations, not causes.

The cohort fingerprint: what goes with each emotion Statistics

The cohort average of every emotion and predictor. Blue = better, orange = worse; ● = its 95% interval excludes 0 (not chance). The next tab shows the same for Lena Demo.

ⓘ About this view
THE MODEL (statistics): a linear mixed model for every emotion and type of question, estimated in two steps.

Step 1, each participant on their own: RIGHT NOW, their average emotion in a situation minus their average over all observations (their usual level); SINCE THE LAST OBSERVATION, a regression of the emotion on the same emotion at the previous observation (at most 24 h earlier) and the predictors of the model together, all centred on the person's own averages, so that someone who is always more anxious does not look anxious "at work".

Step 2, the cohort: the average of these personal effects over the training participants, weighted by how precise each one is, and how much people really differ (a random effect per predictor, DerSimonian-Laird estimator).

This view shows the cohort average: rows = emotions, columns = predictors grouped by model (coloured line on top); left the "right now" models, right the "since the last observation" models. Colour: blue better, orange worse. A dot: its 95% interval excludes 0. Emotions or predictors without data in the cohort are left out. Hover: the interval, how much people differ (SD and I², the share of the spread that is real differences between people) and how many participants had enough data.

How Lena Demo's own data and most participants are combined

Each number on this page combines two sources. This person's own data say, for example, how much more relaxed they are at home than usual; with few observations that number is unsure. Most participants say how much people are usually more relaxed at home, and how much people differ from each other. The page shows a weighted average of the two: the more observations this person has, and the more people differ, the more weight goes to their own data. With few observations, or where everyone reacts alike, the number stays close to most participants. This is the standard way of estimating a personal effect in a mixed model (it is called shrinkage: the personal number is "shrunk" towards the cohort).

One example: tried not to show emotions → sad (reversed)

Lena Demo's own data: -0.24 (28 observations). Most participants: -0.50, and people typically differ by about 0.12. Combined: -0.42, that is 32% own data and 68% most participants. The three curves show how sure each source is: the narrower, the surer. The combined curve lies between the other two, closer to the surer one.

Is the next mood better or worse than usual? ML

A machine-learning model, an Explainable Boosting Machine, learned from the training participants which inputs known before an observation (the emotions at the previous one, the past week, app and game engagement, sleep, the time) go with an overall mood better or worse than the person's usual level (median of their previous 14 days). Five classes: Worse than usual (−1 or more below) · Slightly worse (−0.5 to −1) · As usual (within ±0.5) · Slightly better (+0.5 to +1) · Better than usual (+1 or more above). It is a cohort model: its rules are the same for everyone.

ⓘ About this view
THE MODEL (machine learning): an Explainable Boosting Machine (interpret library), a model that adds up one learned curve per input, so every prediction can be read input by input. It is trained on all the training participants together (a cohort model, the same rules for everyone), with each class weighted so that the rare ones count as much as "as usual".

TARGET: overall mood at an observation minus the person's usual level (median of their previous 14 days, at least 3 observations), in five classes: worse (-1 or more below), slightly worse (-0.5 to -1), as usual (within ±0.5), slightly better (+0.5 to +1), better (+1 or more above).

INPUTS (known before the observation, except the time): five emotions at the previous observation (at most 24 h earlier), median and SD of overall mood in the past 7 days, mood observations in the past 7 days, game played in the past 7 days, sleep last night and over the last 5 nights, weekend, time of day.

EVALUATION: participant-grouped cross-validation (the model is always tested on people it has not seen): balanced accuracy, the average of the five hit rates (20% = guessing), compared with "always as usual" and "same as the last observation". On this person: the same on their own observations.

How good is the model?

Balanced accuracy = the average of the five hit rates, so the many "as usual" observations do not hide the rare ones. 20% = guessing. The model is worth reading only where it beats the two simple rules.

How much data is behind each part

ⓘ About this view
Green = enough data, amber = limited, red = too little for that part to be read for this person. Mood observations: fewer than the eligibility threshold = too few, up to twice = limited. Network: pairs of consecutive observations with all its emotions. EBM: observations with a usual level, and how often sleep is known.
  • 148 mood observations over 42 days
    Fewer than 20 = too few, 20-39 = limited, 40 or more = good.
  • Own estimates for 22 of the 23 situations, events, thoughts and coping strategies
    A situation, event or strategy gets an estimate from this person's own data only if it happened in at least 5 of their observations and did not happen in at least 5 (scores, such as negative thinking: at least 10 answers that vary). Otherwise the page shows the cohort average for it. By model: Where 4/4, Doing 5/5, With 3/4, Events 2/2, Thinking 2/2, Coping 6/6. Cohort average only: online only.
  • 147 pairs of observations for the emotion network
    Consecutive observations at most 24 h apart with all the network's emotions at the earlier one (148 of the 148 observations have all 6). Fewer than 20 = too few, 20-39 = limited, 40 or more = good.
  • 145 observations with a usual level for the EBM
    An observation needs at least 3 earlier observations in the previous 14 days. Fewer than 20: no accuracy for this person; 20-39: limited; 40 or more: good.
  • Sleep known for 95 % of the observations (40 nights)
    From the morning observation. On days without it, the sleep inputs are “missing” (the EBM has a score for that).
  • The EBM is a cohort model
    Its curves are the same for everyone. “This participant” importance shows which inputs move the prediction for them, given their own values, not that they react differently. How this person differs is in the emotion network.
  • This participant was kept out of training
    The accuracy on their observations is a fair test, as for a new patient.

Models built on 2026-10-28 (mixed models) and 2026-10-28 (network and EBM) from 84 training participants.
